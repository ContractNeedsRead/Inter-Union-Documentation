# Workplace Harassment Training

## Purpose

A combined guide from the USPS "Workplace Harassment: What It Is and What Managers Must Do" training deck and the APWU Industrial Relations Department's "Improving Your Workplace Environment" brochure. The USPS deck states management's duties and the legal frame; the APWU brochure states the member's rights and the actions a member can take. Together they are the harassment half of the vault's procedure kit for harassment, EEO, and OWCP cases.

## Content

### What exactly is workplace harassment

Workplace harassment is a form of employment discrimination that violates Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967, and the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990. It includes unwelcome actions and comments directed at an individual because of his or her gender — and covers a wide range of behaviors consisting of words, actions, or both. The harassment must be **objectively hostile** (a reasonable person would find it offensive) and **subjectively offensive** to the harassee (otherwise the conduct would not alter the terms or conditions of employment).

### Recognizing types of workplace harassment

- **Tangible employment action / quid pro quo harassment:** harassment of a subordinate by someone with supervisory authority that results in a tangible employment action — hiring, firing, promoting, demoting, changing work assignments, reassigning. "Quid pro quo" means "something for something": the use of power and authority to alter an employee's job conditions or economic benefits because the employee submits to or refuses unwelcome demands. Elements: the employee is subject to unwelcome advances or conduct, and acceptance or rejection of the harassment is an express or implied condition of a job benefit or the cause of a job detriment. Example: a supervisor conditions promotion on drinks after work; a supervisor fires or demotes a subordinate who rejects demands.
- **Hostile environment harassment:** a pattern of continuing unwelcome behavior that unreasonably interferes with an employee's work performance or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment.

### Forms of harassment

Verbal, non-verbal, graphic, physical, and **third-party** (behavior not directed at the harassee per se but that indirectly affects or offends the person).

### Inappropriate conduct

Postal policy and regulations prohibit inappropriate conduct and conduct that could lead to workplace harassment: preferential treatment of an applicant a manager desires to date violates postal regulations and standards of ethical conduct even if it is not harassment; a manager must not allow employees to provoke colleagues, however seemingly harmless or unintended.

### Legal consequences

- **Quid pro quo:** the employer is **automatically liable** for harassment by managers/supervisors when it culminates in a tangible employment action (demotion, denial of promotion, termination, unfavorable reassignment — an official act inflicting direct economic harm).
- **Hostile environment:** when no tangible action results, the employer can raise a defense by showing (1) it exercised reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct harassing behavior, and (2) the employee unreasonably failed to use the corrective measures provided (e.g., failed to report).

### Reasonable care to prevent and correct

The employer has a policy against harassment; the policy is distributed to all employees; it provides alternative avenues to report, assurances of confidentiality, and no retaliation; employees receive harassment training; investigations are prompt, thorough, and effective; harassers are stopped and corrective action is taken. Employees are jointly responsible because a prompt complaint often prevents the conduct from becoming pervasive or severe.

### Initial Management Inquiry Process (IMIP)

When any person in management receives a harassment complaint, he/she must: take it seriously, meet promptly with the employee, stop the behavior, ensure a full IMIP is done, and take appropriate final action. **What not to do:** do not label the conduct workplace harassment, do not dismiss it as harmless fun, do not tell the employee he/she is overreacting, do not offer personal opinions.

Practical pointers: meet promptly; advise of confidentiality and its limits; listen; ask questions; take notes; advise that retaliation is illegal; advise you will pursue the inquiry and report back; use the checklist and interview guidelines in Publication 552. Determine whether to separate the parties and/or recommend EAP counseling; assess the nature and scope of the problem; with HR (or the next higher level manager for HQ units) decide whether to continue the IMIP or refer it. Weigh all the evidence, assess witness credibility, and consider the totality of circumstances (nature, severity, pervasiveness).

Outcomes:

- **Inconclusive or no harassment:** separately report to the harassee and alleged harasser; advise that retaliation is illegal; follow up; note date and subject in personal notes.
- **Minor, single incident:** resolve and take remedial action; separately report to both; advise retaliation is illegal; follow up; note date and subject.
- **Serious and/or recurring incident:** stop the behavior; consult the Manager, Human Resources (or next higher level manager for HQ units); take remedial action; fully document evidence of actions; advise the harasser of the remedial/disciplinary action and that retaliation is illegal; restore the harassee and achieve resolution; follow up; submit findings under confidential cover. HR retains files for 4 years and conducts an annual review.

### Fixing the problem — remedial action

Corrective action must end the inappropriate conduct: impose progressive discipline as necessary, and consider supplementing it with stand-up talks, training, EAP counseling, and workplace monitoring. The corrective ladder: discussion, remedial training, letter of warning, suspension, transfer, demotion, discharge — discipline in proportion to the severity (minor single event: discussion and/or training; serious recurring conduct: suspension or discharge).

### Practical points for managers

- **Weingarten rule:** bargaining unit employees have the right to union representation, upon request, when the employee has a reasonable belief that discipline could result from the interview; requests and attendance should be documented.
- **Deaf and hard of hearing:** provide a certified interpreter for all stages of the IMIP process.
- Keep all records, notes, and documents confidential and secured; forward documents under confidential cover when the inquiry is complete.
- Read Publication 552 (Manager's Guide to Understanding, Investigating, and Preventing Harassment); its IMIP materials must be used whenever a complaint is brought to management's attention or management becomes aware of possible harassment.

### Your rights if you experience abusive conduct (APWU brochure)

The right to a safe and healthful workplace; to expect supervisors to maintain good employee relations; to a harassment-free work environment; to be free from intimidation, threats, or bullying at any level; to report inappropriate behavior without reprisal; to have reports promptly and properly investigated; to report disturbances or improper actions on postal premises (using PS Form 1767); to report to shop stewards under Article 14 and Publication 552; to file grievances on inappropriate behavior and to have representation in investigatory processes; to file complaints through other forums (EEO, Office of Inspector General, Postal Inspection Service); and to engage in concerted activity with co-workers to end hostile work environments. **Retaliation is illegal** for exercising any of these rights.

Examples of abusive conduct: bullying (repeated verbal, physical, or social behavior misusing power to cause harm), intimidation (conduct making a person fear for job, safety, or livelihood), threats (statements or acts intended to inflict harm or interfere with official duties), harassment (unwelcome behavior someone finds offensive — whistling/catcalling, sexual innuendos, stalking, offensive remarks, sexual advances; productivity demands that lead to stress, anxiety, unsafe work, shortcuts, or unwarranted discipline under Article 34; personal attacks, belittling comments, name-calling, racially derogatory comments; and using employees against each other), and illegal harassment under Title VII, the Rehabilitation Act, the Age Discrimination Act, or other laws.

### Actions you may take (APWU brochure)

Report it, pursue it, don't tolerate it: tell the abuser to STOP (preferably with a witness and/or union representative); keep a written record of dates, times, and witnesses; report on PS Form 1767 with details; report to another supervisor or higher-level manager; report to someone you trust; report to your union representative and follow through the grievance-arbitration procedure; if the behavior rises to a Title VII violation, file through the EEO process; report to the OIG and/or Postal Inspection Service under the PMG workplace harassment policy; request that management initiate an IMIP (Publications 552/553); stand together with co-workers through petitions, class-action grievances, meetings with the boss, and informational picketing.

Be careful what you say or write: on PS Form 1767 avoid the terms "stress," "anxiety," or "hostile work environment" (they may trigger unwarranted medical referral and carry legal connotations); with the OIG avoid wording that could be misconstrued as threatening; call the police only when imminent threat or danger is present; be specific about dates, times, people, and witnesses, and cite applicable USPS regulations; always hold management accountable for all workplace behavior, even from co-workers; understand the IMIP is a process conducted by management, for management, and about management; seek union assistance; be factual and honest.

The brochure includes an incident-notes worksheet (dates, times, location, supervisors, witnesses, detailed facts, applicable policies; whether reported, to whom, whether the abuser was told to stop, whether a PS Form 1767 was filed and with whom, whether other processes were used, and whether a grievance was filed).
